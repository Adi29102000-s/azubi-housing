How much cash to land with

There are two legal limits, and neither of them is your real problem. The real problem is that the money in your blocked account isn't available on the day you arrive — and what you're carrying has to cover everything until it is.

Your blocked account doesn't unlock when you land. It unlocks when you register your address — and appointments are booked out weeks.

01

The two legal limits

One on the Indian side, one on the European side. Both are real, both are easy to comply with, and neither is the thing that catches people out.

Leaving India — USD 3,000

The RBI allows travellers to purchase foreign currency notes or coins only up to USD 3,000 per visit. Anything beyond that has to travel as a forex card, a bank transfer, or traveller's cheques.

Higher limits exist for a handful of countries — Iraq and Libya at USD 5,000, and Iran, Russia and the CIS republics at the full USD 250,000. Germany is not one of them.

Entering the EU — €10,000

If you carry €10,000 or more, or the equivalent in any currency, you must declare it to customs on arrival. That's an EU-wide rule, not a German one.

"Cash" here includes banknotes, coins, traveller's cheques and gold. Failing to declare means penalties, and customs can intervene below the threshold too if something looks wrong.

In practice

No student is anywhere near either ceiling. USD 3,000 is roughly €2,600 — several times what you should be carrying. The €10,000 declaration rule will never apply to you. Know both so you can stop worrying about them.

The number that actually matters

Somewhere between €300 and €500 in cash, plus a working card. That's not a rule — it's what covers a normal first two to three weeks in a normal German university town. The rest of this page is how that number is built, so you can adjust it for your own situation instead of guessing.

02

Why your money is locked

A blocked account is not a bank account you can spend from on day one. It releases money only after a chain of steps, and every step has a queue.

Step 1

You land

You have your passport, your visa, and whatever cash and cards are in your bag. Nothing else is usable yet.

Step 2

You need an address

A permanent one, with a landlord willing to sign a Wohnungsgeberbestätigung. A hostel won't do it. This alone can take weeks.

Step 3

You register — Anmeldung

An appointment at the Bürgeramt. In most cities the earliest free slot is weeks away, and you cannot register without one.

Step 4

Money starts flowing

Only now does the chain complete: registration, then a current account, then your blocked account pays out monthly into it.

Say it accurately

Timelines vary a lot by provider and by city — some people are set up in under two weeks, others take two months. Don't plan around a best case. Plan for the cash you're carrying to last until step four completes, whenever that is.

This is why "how much cash should I bring" is the wrong question. The right one is: how many weeks do I need to survive on what's in my bag? Everything on the rest of this page follows from that.

The one that isn't on this list

Your Kaution — the rental deposit, capped by law at three months' cold rent. That is not landing money and should never come out of the cash in your bag. It goes by bank transfer, after you have signed a contract, and never before. Anyone asking for a deposit in cash before a signature is a warning sign.

Add your own rows up and you land in the €300–500 range for most people, more if you're arriving without accommodation. That's the whole derivation — there's no magic figure, just a list.

04

How to split your money

Four places, four jobs. Putting everything in one of them is the mistake.

  • 01Cash in euros — €300–500For the first days, the SIM, small shops, and anything that refuses a foreign card. Split it: some in your wallet, some in your hand luggage, some in your suitcase. Never all in one bag.
  • 02Forex card — the bulk of your travel moneyLoaded in euros before you fly, so exchange-rate movement can't hurt you mid-trip. This is your main spending method until a German account exists.
  • 03A backup card — separate from the firstAn international debit or credit card, stored somewhere different. One card blocked on a Sunday with no backup is a genuinely bad weekend.
  • 04Your Indian account — kept liveYou'll still need it for OTPs, for topping up the forex card remotely, and for anything unfinished at home. Tell the bank you're travelling so they don't freeze it on foreign activity.
Before you fly

Test your forex card at an ATM in India, and check it is enabled for international use and for both ATM withdrawals and point-of-sale. Finding out it isn't, at an airport in a country where you have no phone signal, is the scenario this whole page exists to prevent.

05

Choosing a card

No recommendation here on purpose. Rates and eligibility change constantly and depend on your bank — but the questions to ask don't change.

What to compare, in this order

  • The exchange rate applied at loading. Not the advertised rate — the actual one on the day. This is where most of the cost hides, and it dwarfs the visible fees.
  • Issuance and loading fees. One-off, and per reload.
  • ATM withdrawal fee abroad. Charged per withdrawal, so fewer, larger withdrawals cost less. The German ATM may add its own charge on top.
  • Cross-currency markup. If your card is loaded in euros and you spend in euros, this shouldn't apply. Confirm it doesn't.
  • Reload method. Can you top it up online from Germany, or does someone have to visit a branch in India?
  • What happens to leftover balance. Encashment fee, and the rate you get back.
Not financial advice

This page names no card and no bank. Compare at least two on the day you buy, read the actual fee schedule rather than the marketing page, and confirm what you'll be charged before you load money onto anything.

One thing to check that people forget

RuPay does not work in Europe. If your Indian debit card is RuPay-only, it is not a backup — it is a piece of plastic. Make sure your backup card is Visa or Mastercard, and that international usage is switched on in your bank's app before you leave.

06

Where you'll still need physical cash

Card acceptance in Germany has improved a lot, but it is still noticeably behind India — and the gaps cluster in exactly the places you'll be in during week one.

Bakeries and small kiosks

Often cash-only, or card only above a minimum spend of €5 or €10.

Spätis and corner shops

Where you'll buy your SIM starter pack. Frequently cash-only.

Weekly markets

Fruit, vegetables, cheese. Almost entirely cash.

Some doctors and clinics

For small fees and anything not covered by insurance.

Laundry machines in dorms

Coins or a dorm-specific card. Not your Visa.

Pfand and vending machines

Small change, constantly. Keep coins.

None of these are large amounts. The point is frequency: you'll be reaching for cash several times a day in your first fortnight, which is why a working card alone isn't enough.

07

Traps

The specific ways the money part goes wrong.

Airport currency exchange

The worst rates you will encounter anywhere. Load your euros in India, or withdraw from a normal bank ATM in town.

"Pay in your home currency?"

Always decline. Choosing INR at a German terminal lets the machine set its own exchange rate, and it is never in your favour.

All your cash in one bag

One lost bag, no money, no phone plan, no bank account. Split it across three places.

A RuPay-only backup

Doesn't work in Europe at all. Confirm your backup is Visa or Mastercard.

Not telling your Indian bank

Foreign transactions on an account with no travel notice get flagged and frozen, usually on the worst possible day.

Assuming the blocked account is instant

It isn't. It's the last link in a chain that starts with finding an address.

Many small ATM withdrawals

Most forex cards charge per withdrawal. Two large withdrawals cost far less than ten small ones.

Paying a deposit in cash

Kaution goes by transfer, after signing. A cash deposit before a contract is how deposits disappear.

08

Questions

Is it illegal to carry more than USD 3,000 in cash?

The rule is about how much foreign currency you're permitted to purchase in note form for a trip — up to USD 3,000 per visit for Germany. The rest of your allowance travels as a forex card or transfer. It's a limit on the cash form of your forex, not a criminal threshold, but stay inside it and carry your exchange receipts.

Do I have to declare my money at German customs?

Only at €10,000 or above, including cash, traveller's cheques and gold. Below that, no declaration is needed. You will not be near this.

How long does a blocked account actually take to become usable?

It varies widely — provider, city, and how fast you find an address all change the answer. The dependency that dominates is your Anmeldung, and appointment waits differ hugely between cities. Budget for weeks, be pleased if it's days.

Can I open a German bank account before I register my address?

Some online banks allow it, most traditional ones don't. Even where an account opens, the card arrives by post — which needs a stable address. Don't build your plan on it.

Should I bring US dollars or euros?

Euros. Converting dollars to euros in Germany costs you a second spread on top of the first, and airport exchange counters are the worst place to do it.

How much cash can I bring back to India?

A resident returning from abroad may bring in Indian currency notes up to ₹25,000. That's a rule about coming back, not about leaving — they're separate things and are often confused.

Is Germany really that cash-dependent?

Less than it used to be — most supermarkets, chains and restaurants take cards now. But bakeries, kiosks, markets and dorm laundry rooms often don't, and those are exactly where you'll spend in week one.

What if I run out before my account works?

You can usually top up a forex card remotely from India, so keep that channel open and make sure someone at home can do it. That's the fallback — not carrying more cash.
