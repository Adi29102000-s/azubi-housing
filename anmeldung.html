The document nobody warns you about

Without this, you cannot register. Full stop.

Wohnungsgeberbestätigung

A signed declaration from your landlord confirming you live at the address and when you moved in. Your rental contract is not a substitute. The Bürgeramt cannot process your registration without this specific form, no matter what else you bring.

Who can sign it: your landlord, the property management company, or — if you're in a WG or sublet — the main tenant, with the landlord's knowledge. For a student dorm, the housing office signs it. Ask them for it before you move in.

Your landlord is legally obliged to provide it in writing, and refusing is a fineable offence they can be reported for. Templates are downloadable from every city's portal.

Ask before you pay Short-term furnished lets and Airbnbs often refuse to issue it, because it exposes them to tourism tax questions. If a place can't give you this form, you can't register there — so ask before you book, not after.

The other trap: hotels and hostels don't count. You need an address where you actually live and can receive post. You can stay in temporary accommodation while you search — you just can't register from it.

How it goes

Roughly 15 minutes with the clerk, once you get there. Getting there is the hard part.

  1. Book the appointment the day you sign

    Not the day you move in — the day you sign the lease. Berlin, Munich and Frankfurt routinely book out weeks or months ahead, which makes the 14-day deadline mathematically impossible. Booking early is your defence. If you can show you booked within 14 days but the slot was months out, clerks generally won't fine you.

  2. Get the Wohnungsgeberbestätigung signed

    Download the template from your city's Bürgeramt page and hand it to your landlord. Bring the original to the appointment if you can, not a photocopy.

  3. Fill in the Anmeldeformular beforehand

    It's in German. Download it, translate it, complete it at home. Doing it in the waiting room under pressure is how mistakes happen.

  4. Go to the appointment

    Arrive five minutes early with documents organised. Don't assume the clerk speaks English — many don't. Bring a German-speaking friend if you're unsure, or at least have your form completed so little needs explaining.

  5. Walk out with the Meldebescheinigung

    A single stamped A4 sheet with your name, address and registration date. It's issued on the spot, free. Photograph it before you leave the building and ask for extra copies — you'll be handing it over repeatedly for months.

What to bring

Missing one means a second appointment
  • Passport with visaNon-EU nationals must bring a passport. A residence permit doesn't substitute for it at this stage.
  • Wohnungsgeberbestätigung, signedThe one that decides everything. Original if possible.
  • Completed AnmeldeformularFrom your city's portal, filled in before you arrive.
  • Appointment confirmationSome cities issue a reference number (Vorgangsnummer). Bring it.
  • Rental contractNot always requested, but bring it anyway.
  • Marriage or birth certificatesOnly if registering family members. One form each.

What it unlocks

Everything downstream

  • Tax ID (Steueridentifikationsnummer)Issued automatically and posted to your registered address, usually within two to four weeks. You don't apply for it separately.
  • Bank accountMost traditional banks want proof of address before they'll open one.
  • Health insurance activationYour insurer needs the certificate to confirm your cover to the university.
  • University enrolmentUsually requires the registration certificate alongside proof of insurance.
  • Residence permitThe Ausländerbehörde will ask for it.
  • Semester ticket, library card, phone contractAll the small things that make daily life work.

Questions people actually ask

I've missed the 14 days. Am I fined?

Usually not. Fines are rarely applied for honest delays, especially when no appointment slots were available. Book anyway and keep proof of when you booked. The practical consequences of not registering — no bank account, no insurance, no residence permit — hurt far more than the fine risk.

Can I register from a hostel or Airbnb?

Generally no. You need an address where you live and receive post, and most short-term accommodation won't issue the landlord confirmation. Some furnished-let platforms flag which listings allow registration — filter for it.

Can I do it online?

If you're arriving from abroad for the first time, no. Berlin and Hamburg have launched digital registration, but it's limited to German or EU citizens with an activated eID who are already registered somewhere in Germany and moving within the country. Munich is fully in person.

I'm in a WG. Who signs my form?

The main tenant can sign it, with the landlord's knowledge. In a student dorm, the housing administration signs it.

My landlord won't give me the form.

They're legally required to. Print the city template, hand it to them, and mention that refusing is reportable to the Bürgeramt and carries a penalty. If they still refuse, that's a serious warning sign about the tenancy itself.

I'm moving flats. Do I do this again?

Yes. It's called Ummeldung, and the same 14-day rule applies from your new move-in date. New address, new landlord confirmation, same process.

What actually goes wrong

  • Booking the appointment after moving in instead of after signing. Those weeks are the difference between registering on time and registering in month two.
  • Arriving with the rental contract but no Wohnungsgeberbestätigung. The single most common wasted appointment.
  • Booking a short-term let that can't issue the form. Ask before you pay.
  • Not putting your name on the letterbox. Your tax ID arrives by post to your registered address. No name on the box, and it goes back.
  • Keeping only one copy of the Meldebescheinigung. Photograph it immediately and ask for spares.
  • Assuming the clerk speaks English. Many don't. Prepare the form in advance.

Check your own city's rules before you go

Procedures, appointment waits and enforcement vary considerably by city and change over time. The legal basis is the Bundesmeldegesetz, but your local Bürgeramt is the authority on your case — check their page for the current form, the booking system and the documents they ask for.
