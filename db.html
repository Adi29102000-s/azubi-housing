    02

    The full booking process

    Do this in India, on wifi, with time to think — not at the airport with a dead SIM.

    1. Download DB NavigatorDeutsche Bahn's official app, free, and available in English — set the language on first open. This is the only app you need. Avoid third-party resellers.
    2. Create an accountUse your own email. An account keeps the ticket inside the app, which matters when you have no printer and no signal.
    3. Search the airport station, not the cityThis is the mistake that costs people an hour. "Frankfurt(M) Flughafen Fernbf" is not "Frankfurt(Main)Hbf" — they're on opposite sides of the city. The planner above gives you the exact station name for your airport.
    4. Add a 2–3 hour buffer after landingImmigration, baggage, and finding your platform all take longer on your first day than you expect.
    5. Pick the ICE, not the REICE is the fast long-distance train. RE and RB are regional — far slower, more changes, and miserable with two suitcases.
    6. Select 2. KlasseSecond class. It's the affordable one and it's what students travel in. First class is a separate, much pricier ticket.
    7. Consider a seat reservationA few euros. On a long ICE in September with luggage, worth every cent — without one you may stand.
    8. Pay and download the ticket offlineInternational cards work. Download it inside the app so it opens with no internet, then screenshot it as backup.
    On the train

    A conductor will check tickets. Show the ticket in the app plus the passport you booked with — the name has to match. Have both ready before they reach you.

    03

    Which ticket type

    This choice matters more than the price gap suggests.

    TypePriceLocked to one train?Use it when
    Super SparpreisCheapestYesYou have a big buffer after landing
    SparpreisCheapYesSame, with a small rebooking fee
    FlexpreisDearestNoTight connection, or a flight you don't trust
    The trade-off

    Sparpreis tickets are tied to the exact train you booked. Flight late, train missed, ticket gone — you buy a new one. Either leave a proper buffer of two to three hours, or pay more for Flexpreis. On a first arrival the buffer is usually the better answer.

    And the Deutschlandticket

    €63 a month, covering every regional train, S-Bahn, bus and tram in Germany — but not ICE, IC or EC. Buy it for your first weeks in your own city, because your semester ticket won't be active until you enrol. It will not carry you across the country.

    04

    Second class — and how not to end up in first

    This happens on day one, with luggage, in an unfamiliar station.

    Book 2. Klasse

    Second class. Tables, luggage racks, and what almost every student travels in. First class costs far more and gives you very little you actually need on a two-hour journey.

    Spot first class before boarding

    A large 1 on the carriage door and window, and a yellow stripe along the outside. Second class carries a 2. The platform display also shows which section is which.

    If you sit in the wrong one

    Travelling in first class on a second-class ticket counts as not having a valid ticket for that carriage. The conductor will charge you the fare difference plus a penalty. "I didn't know" is not accepted, and it's an expensive first hour in the country.

    Look up at the carriage number before you step on. It takes one second — and with a heavy suitcase you do not want to be walking through a moving train to change carriages.

    05

    What's different at each airport

    The one thing to know before you land.

    Frankfurt (FRA)

    Has its own long-distance station — the Fernbahnhof — inside the airport. You board an ICE without ever entering the city. There's a separate regional station too, so check which one your train leaves from.

    Munich (MUC)

    No long-distance station. Take the S1 or S8 to München Hbf first, roughly 40–45 minutes, then your ICE. Budget that leg when you book.

    Düsseldorf (DUS)

    Own long-distance station, reached by SkyTrain from the terminals. Very convenient for anywhere in NRW.

    Berlin (BER)

    Station directly under Terminal 1. Regional and some long-distance. Most journeys change at Berlin Hbf or Südkreuz.

    Hamburg (HAM)

    S1 to Hamburg Hbf, around 25 minutes, then long-distance from there. No ICE from the airport itself.

    Cologne/Bonn (CGN)

    Own station under the terminal. Handy for Cologne, Bonn and Aachen — and many of those routes are fully regional, so the Deutschlandticket covers them.

    06

    Questions

    Should I really book before I fly?

    Yes. It's cheaper, and you won't be at a ticket machine with no data and no idea which platform. Just leave two to three hours after landing, or pay for a flexible fare if the connection is tight.

    What if my flight is delayed and I miss the train?

    A Sparpreis is tied to that train, so normally you'd buy a new ticket — which is exactly why the buffer matters. If it happens, go to the DB Reisezentrum, the service centre in every main station. A person will help you more than a machine will.

    Can I use the Deutschlandticket for this?

    Only if the whole route runs on regional trains — the planner above tells you per route. It is never valid on ICE, IC or EC.

    Is there a luggage limit on German trains?

    No limit and no fee for normal suitcases. But there's no luggage van either — bags go on overhead racks or in the racks at the carriage ends, and those fill up. Board early, and avoid the last carriage.

    Do I need a seat reservation?

    Not required, but for a long ICE in September with two suitcases it's worth the few euros. Without one you may be standing.

    What if my train is cancelled?

    Common enough. If the delay is DB's fault, the train binding on a Sparpreis is lifted and you can take the next service. Check the app or ask staff.

    Will announcements be in English?

    On ICE, usually. On regional trains, often not. Follow the app rather than the announcements, and watch the display above the door for the next stop.
